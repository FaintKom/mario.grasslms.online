Security and Privacy Essentials, a six-unit course with Unit 1 built

Security awareness training usually arrives as a forty-minute slide deck with a quiz at the end. I designed this course as six short units that each stand on their own. From Unit 2 onwards, every unit opens by asking the learner to use something from an earlier one, so the spacing between units does the work of revision. This page sets out the outcomes, the units, the experience map and the assessment. Unit 1 is built in full and runs in a lesson shell that opens each step only after the previous one is finished.

Unit 1 takes about nine minutes on a computer or a phone. The film needs the sound on, and you don't need to sign up.

The course is for all staff and takes about thirty minutes in total at the learner's own pace. It is delivered as SCORM 1.2 and on the mobile web, so a learner can stop and pick it up again later. I was the learning experience designer and did both the design and the build. The design follows backward design, with measurable outcomes written in ABCD form and tagged with Bloom levels. Assessment is constructively aligned with the outcomes (Biggs), and evaluation uses Kirkpatrick's four levels. The content draws on the published texts of ISO 27001, 27002 and 27701, the GDPR and NIS2.

The brief and the design decisions

ISO 27001 clause 7.3 requires everyone who works under the organisation's control to know the security policy and what it means if they don't follow it. ISO 27002 §6.3 describes the awareness training that gets them there. GDPR Art.32(4) requires the controller and the processor to make sure that anyone with access to personal data processes it only on their instructions. For essential and important entities under NIS2, Art.20(2) requires the members of the management body to follow cybersecurity training and asks Member States to encourage those entities to offer similar training to their staff on a regular basis. Art.21(2)(g) lists basic cyber hygiene practices and cybersecurity training among the risk-management measures those entities have to take. On an ordinary working day, employees meet phishing, unlabelled data, unlocked screens and incidents nobody reports. A policy PDF does little to change how they handle any of it.

I counted the course as a success only if it produced the behaviours the organisation needs and can show to an auditor. Every unit is set in the learner's own inbox, desk and working week, because training that ignores daily work gets clicked through without changing anything.

The course is for everyone, whether they work in the office, from home or on the front line. Digital fluency differs from person to person, no security vocabulary is assumed and nobody has much time to spare. Because prior knowledge is so uneven, the design keeps the way in easy. It also tries to show each learner within the first twenty seconds why the topic matters to them.

Training can build recognition, good small decisions and a habit of reporting. It cannot replace technical controls or fix a culture that punishes reporting, so I raised those two points with the control owner as conditions the environment has to meet.

The six units and how each one reuses the last

Each unit has a single objective, lasts between three and nine minutes and works on its own. The format changes from one unit to the next so that no two in a row feel the same. Spacing comes from a chain of retrieval instead of a calendar. From Unit 2 onwards, each unit opens by asking the learner to use earlier material in a new decision instead of reading it again.

To keep the load manageable, each unit has one objective and a worked example comes before independent practice. The labels for the tells sit on the message itself, so attention never has to split between two places. Scenes are visual with short text, and the narration never repeats the words on screen. The whole course fits into sessions the length of a commute.

How the course is assessed

Learners are assessed against criteria and never ranked against each other. Unit 6 gives five new scenarios that each combine several outcomes. A learner passes with at least four of the five, as long as the critical item is correct. The critical item asks them to report a suspected breach of personal data straight away, even though they caused it. Under the GDPR the organisation has 72 hours to notify the supervisory authority of a personal data breach that puts people at risk, and an entity covered by NIS2 has 24 hours to send an early warning about a significant incident. A breach that someone keeps quiet about uses up that time before anyone can act on it, so knowing the password rules cannot make up for getting this item wrong. A learner who fails goes back to the unit that owns the outcome and gets one more attempt at a variant.

The three probes at the start of Unit 1 are diagnostic. They come before any teaching and are not scored, and each answer shows the learner how far a first impression can be trusted before the film explains the case. Every unit is formative practice in making decisions, with as many retries as the learner wants. The feedback explains the tell behind each verdict. Only Unit 6 is summative.

Evaluation follows Kirkpatrick's four levels. Two exit questions cover level 1, and the criterion pass covers level 2, with the pass rate on the critical item reported separately. At level 3 I look at the click rate and report rate in phishing simulations and at how long people take to report real incidents. Level 4 is the share of all incidents caught early, and it is level 3 that decides what gets redesigned.

Unit 1 as it is built

Unit 1 runs in a lesson shell I built, with a list of steps, a progress bar and progress saved on the learner's device. Each step opens only when the one before it is finished: the three probes answered, the film watched to the end, every tell in the inbox flagged without a false alarm, and five of the six final questions right.

  • The unit opens with three messages that the learner trusts or reports before anything has been explained. These answers are not scored, and each one explains the tell behind it.
  • A film of about two minutes works through the four tells scene by scene. Near the start it pauses for a few seconds so the learner can pick out the fake email.
  • In the inbox simulator the learner opens three messages and flags the nine tells hidden in them. Every message also has normal parts, and flagging one counts as a false alarm, so clicking everything does not pass. A second attempt keeps the flags that were correct.
  • A longread of about 450 words explains the four tells to a reader who is short of time.
  • Six questions after the lesson check the four tells, and each answer explains why it is right or wrong.
  • The unit closes with one habit to keep and a private question about which message came closest to fooling the learner this week.
